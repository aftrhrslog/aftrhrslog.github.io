---
title: My unhealthy obsession with constant self-improvement
date: 2020-04-01T19:00:00+09:00
tags:
  - Thoughts
---

For the past few months, I haven't been living life to the fullest because I've been preoccupied with the idea that I should focus my time on building a better career. I've always been <mark>hard-wired</mark> to want to "do more" and "do better." It has been a <mark>recurring</mark> problem in my thirties, something which now <mark>haunts</mark> me.

I wasn't a self-help enthusiast in my twenties. Looking back at my college years, whenever a semester would end, <mark>mindlessly</mark> <mark>lazing around</mark> all day became my routine. Nowadays, I believe every minute should be spent doing something productive, and I want to practice that belief in my daily life. It may be this attitude towards life that has helped me adapt wall to the extremely competitive Korean society.

Whenever I get spare time, rather than enjoy it, I <mark>fill it with plans</mark>. I <mark>get restless</mark> and <mark>feel guilty</mark> if I don't do anything on my day off. Even if I have nothing particular to do on that day, I look for something that I can do to <mark>upskill</mark> in some way. I push myself to sit at my desk and learn English or read papers or textbooks. I can't relax without <mark>feeling consumed with guilt</mark>. If I <mark>succumb</mark> to my physical need for rest, I turn on the TV and lie down on the sofa. Even then, I have to ignore <mark>my inner voice</mark> telling me I'm lazy. It sounds something like this typically,

"You're wasting time. While you watch TV and take rest, someone else is publishing a paper in a great journal. Someone else is mastering a foreign language. You're going to <mark>fall behind</mark>."

In recent years, I stopped listening to music while driving; instead, I began listening to English-language radio news to improve my English listening skills. I didn't even allow myself the time to listen to music when I <mark>felt down</mark> and needed <mark>a pick-me-up</mark>. Radio news channels are mostly world affairs, so they aren't helpful for my mood. My obsession with being productive doesn't allow me to take time for myself. I even carry a notebook, an iPad, and pens with me in case I get time to kill. I get too <mark>caught up</mark> in following the rules and guidelines I've <mark>imposed</mark> on myself; I focus too much on what's possible in the future, rather than <mark>savoring</mark> the precious present.

My friends often say that I'm an inspiration because although I've already achieved so much, I keep going. I know my hard work motivates them to do better. However, becoming the ideal version of myself is a fantasy. If I had been a true <mark>self-help guru</mark>, I would have demonstrated some spectacular and impressive achievements. Obviously, I haven't.

In reality, I don't feel special; I feel like an ordinary person because I get <mark>deflated</mark> a lot when I look at people who have achieved more than me. I judge myself for not being in that position, yet. I can be entirely dominated by my insecurities, which is why my obsession with self-improvement stems from the idea that my best is never good enough. In this type of situation, I don't know any other method to gain <mark>self-worth</mark> but through achieving goals and continuously seeking approval. However, every time I've accomplished something, I immediately compare it against what other people have achieved; I <mark>play it down</mark> and begin thinking about the next challenge. That's the secret of my constant self-improvement, and it's a very unhealthy habit. Along the way, there are moments when I feel satisfied with my results, but they don't last. With this relentless but <mark>short-sighted</mark> obsession with personal improvement, I will never be like the great people I admire.

Now, I'm determined to choose a different path. Pushing boundaries to be like someone else is not the way of living life. Instead, I'm going to <mark>pat</mark> myself on the back and take time to appreciate what I've been through to get to where I am now. Before setting a goal, I'll ask myself, "Is that what you want? What is the most satisfying choice for you right now?" Approval from others won't guarantee my happiness; only I can do that. Going after what I want is what matters. I'm still scared of falling behind, however, <mark>what's the point of</mark> improving if I'm not enjoying myself along the way? As a first step, I'll make a compromise between what this competitive society expects from me and what I love to do, and I'm <mark>resolved</mark> not to feel guilty about this choice. I don't need any excuse to pursue my happiness.
